# Chapter 5: Building a Coherent Guide With AI

The first four chapters introduced three useful lenses for understanding products, messages, and design:

- **Persuasion** asks: What response are we trying to enable?
- **Brand archetypes** ask: What meaning or identity are we expressing?
- **Visual design language** asks: How should that meaning look and feel?

These lenses can also help people direct AI-assisted creative and technical work. AI can generate text, images, code, layouts, and ideas quickly. However, speed does not automatically create good work. People still need to define the goal, set boundaries, review results, and make responsible decisions.

## Three Lenses for Directing AI

When someone asks an AI to create something, the request should be more thoughtful than “Make me a website” or “Write a report.” A useful prompt gives the AI a goal, audience, tone, constraints, and required output.

### Persuasion: What Response Are We Trying to Enable?

Persuasion helps define the response the work should support.

For example, a student website may need to help a visitor:

- Understand a topic.
- Trust the information.
- Complete a form.
- Sign up for an event.
- Make a responsible decision.
- Feel interested enough to keep reading.

A good AI prompt can explain the desired response. Instead of asking an AI to “make a product page,” a designer can ask it to create a clear product page that helps students compare options without using pressure or misleading claims.

### Archetype: What Meaning or Identity Are We Expressing?

An archetype helps define the personality and meaning of the work.

For example:

- An **Explorer** style may feel active, open, curious, and independent.
- A **Caregiver** style may feel supportive, reassuring, and practical.
- A **Creator** style may feel imaginative, personal, and expressive.
- A **Sage** style may feel knowledgeable, calm, and evidence-focused.

If an AI is creating a website, poster, product description, or app interface, the archetype can guide the tone, language, colors, imagery, and examples.

For example, a project for a tutoring center may use a Caregiver and Sage combination. The content should feel clear, supportive, and trustworthy rather than aggressive or overly trendy.

### Design Language: How Should Meaning Look and Feel?

Design language translates meaning into visible choices.

It includes:

- Typography
- Color
- Layout
- Spacing
- Images
- Icons
- Motion
- Tone of voice
- Information hierarchy

For example, a modernist or Swiss-influenced design language may use a grid, sans-serif type, open space, direct headings, and clear buttons. A postmodern style may use expressive type, unusual layouts, collage, bold color, and playful disruption.

When working with AI, designers should explain not only what they want made, but also what it should feel like and why.

## AI Needs a Specification

A specification is a clear description of what needs to be created. It gives AI a boundary.

Without a specification, an AI system may make assumptions, miss important requirements, create inconsistent output, or produce something that looks impressive but does not solve the real problem.

A useful specification can include:

- The goal of the work.
- The intended audience.
- The desired response from the audience.
- The archetype or brand meaning.
- The visual or writing style.
- Required sections, files, features, or formatting.
- Technical limits.
- Ethical rules.
- What the AI should not do.
- How the result will be checked.

For example, instead of writing:

> “Make a website for a white T-shirt.”

A stronger specification would say:

> “Create a product page for an ordinary high-quality white T-shirt. The audience is college students who value simple, dependable clothing. Use a restrained Swiss-influenced visual style with a grid, clean sans-serif type, generous white space, clear product information, and no misleading scarcity claims. Include a product description, sizing section, care information, and a clear purchase button.”

The second instruction gives the AI a clear goal and limits. It connects persuasion, archetype, and design language.

## Deterministic Checks and Probabilistic Review

Not every review method works in the same way.

### Deterministic Checks

A deterministic check gives the same result every time when the input is the same. These checks are useful because they are cheap, repeatable, and clear.

Examples include:

- Does the required file exist?
- Is the filename correct?
- Does a Markdown file contain a required heading?
- Does a link work?
- Does code compile?
- Does a test pass?
- Does a form require an email address?
- Does a Mermaid diagram use valid syntax?

For this mini textbook, a deterministic check could confirm that all five chapter files exist inside the `book` folder.

### Probabilistic AI Review

AI review can be helpful, but it is probabilistic. That means it may give different answers at different times, and it can make mistakes.

An AI reviewer can help identify:

- Missing sections.
- Unclear wording.
- Repetitive sentences.
- Possible tone problems.
- Accessibility concerns.
- Confusing instructions.
- Potential ethical risks.

However, AI review should not be treated as final proof that something is correct, truthful, fair, safe, or appropriate. AI can misunderstand context, miss errors, or sound confident when it is wrong.
